Drupal Already Manages Content. So What Does AI Actually Add?

By Karthik Kumar D K | Published: Oct 6, 2026 8:11 AM IST
By Karthik Kumar D K , 6 October 2026
Drupal Already Manages Content. So What Does AI Actually Add?

AI is becoming part of almost every software platform, and content management systems are no exception. Drupal is no different. But there is an important question that often gets lost in the excitement around AI: if Drupal already manages content so well, what exactly does AI add?

Drupal has been doing the fundamental work of a content management system for years. It can store articles, pages, documents, products, profiles, policies, events and almost any other type of structured content. It can organise that content using fields, taxonomies and relationships. It gives different users different permissions, supports editorial workflows and approvals, and delivers published content to visitors through websites, APIs and other channels.

So AI is not arriving because Drupal suddenly stopped being capable.

The more interesting question is what happens around the CMS itself. A large Drupal website can contain thousands or even millions of pieces of content, and while Drupal can store and organise all of that information, people still have to create it, review it, rewrite it, translate it, classify it and find the information they need.

That is where AI starts becoming useful.

Drupal Already Does the Important CMS Work

It helps to separate the traditional job of a CMS from the work that people perform around it.

At its simplest, Drupal can store content, structure content, let people create and edit it, control who can publish it, and deliver it to visitors.

Imagine a university running a Drupal website with 100,000 pages. There could be course information, faculty profiles, admission requirements, research papers, department pages, event information, policies, news articles and thousands of old documents.

Drupal can manage that content.

It can tell the system that a particular piece of content is a course, that another is a faculty profile and that another is a policy document. It can connect those pieces together and control who is allowed to edit or publish them.

But Drupal does not automatically remove the human work involved in understanding all of that information.

Someone still has to write the first draft of an article. Someone has to improve an awkward paragraph. Someone may need to summarise a long document. Someone has to decide which category or taxonomy terms apply to a piece of content. Someone has to translate it into another language.

And when an editor is looking for one particular piece of information somewhere inside 100,000 pages, traditional keyword search can only go so far.

This is the area where AI changes the equation.

AI Can Help Create and Improve Content

One of the most obvious uses of AI in Drupal is also one of the most practical: helping editors work with text while they are creating or editing content.

Instead of opening another AI application, copying a paragraph into it, asking for a rewrite and then copying the result back into Drupal, AI can be brought directly into the editing experience.

Drupal's AI CKEditor integration is designed for exactly this kind of work. It can generate text, modify selected text using a prompt, change tone, translate selected content, summarise text, correct spelling and grammar, and reformat HTML.

That changes the workflow in a small but meaningful way.

An editor could write a rough paragraph and ask AI to make it clearer. They could select a long section and ask for a shorter version. They could generate a starting point for an article instead of staring at a completely empty editor.

The important point is that AI does not need to become the publisher.

The editor can still decide whether the generated content is correct, whether it matches the organisation's tone and whether it should actually be published. Drupal continues to handle the content, permissions and publishing process.

AI simply reduces some of the manual work inside that process.

AI Can Also Help Editors Review Content

Creating content is only one part of the job. Large websites also spend a considerable amount of time reviewing and improving content that already exists.

A page might be difficult to read. A title might be unclear. A long article might need a summary. Content may need appropriate taxonomy terms before it can be properly organised.

Drupal's AI Content Suggestions module addresses several of these specific editorial tasks. It can provide summaries, evaluate readability, moderate content, suggest taxonomy terms, suggest titles and alter the tone of content.

This is a useful distinction because AI does not need to take over the entire editorial workflow to be valuable.

Suppose an organisation has 20,000 articles. An editor does not necessarily need AI to independently decide what every article should say. What they may need is assistance with repetitive editorial tasks.

Instead of manually creating a summary for every article, AI can suggest one.

Instead of manually checking whether content is difficult to read, AI can provide a readability assessment.

Instead of deciding taxonomy terms from scratch for every piece of content, an editor can start with AI suggestions and make the final decision.

The human still controls the result. AI reduces the amount of repetitive work required to get there.

Search Is Where AI Could Change Drupal More Fundamentally

Content creation is an obvious use of AI, but search may be an even more interesting one.

Traditional website search generally depends heavily on words. If a visitor searches for a particular term, the search system looks for relevant content containing that term or related indexed information.

That works well when visitors know exactly what they are looking for.

But people do not always search that way.

Consider a university website again. A student might search for:

"I want to study computer science but I don't know whether I need mathematics."

That is not necessarily the same as searching for the exact words used in the university's pages.

The useful information could be spread across admission requirements, course pages, eligibility rules and departmental information.

This is where semantic search becomes interesting. Instead of treating search primarily as a matching exercise between keywords, an AI-powered search system can attempt to understand the meaning behind a query and find content that is conceptually related.

Drupal's AI Search module is being developed for this purpose. It integrates AI-based semantic and vector search with Drupal's Search API and also provides a foundation for retrieval-augmented generation, commonly known as RAG.

There is an important qualification here: AI Search is currently an alpha release, so it should not be presented as though AI-powered semantic search is already a completely mature replacement for every Drupal search implementation.

But the direction is significant.

The traditional question is:

"Which pages contain these words?"

The AI-assisted question becomes:

"Which information on this website is relevant to what this person is asking?"

That is a much bigger change than simply adding a chatbot to a website.

Translation Becomes Part of the CMS Workflow

Multilingual websites provide another clear example.

Drupal already has a strong content translation system. The difficult part is not necessarily storing multiple language versions of content. The difficult part can be producing those translations at scale.

Imagine a government website with 5,000 pages that needs to maintain content in several languages.

A human translation process can be slow and expensive, particularly when content changes frequently.

Drupal's AI Translate module adds AI-assisted translation directly into Drupal's content translation workflow. It can work with translatable fields and can also handle things such as summaries, link titles, image alternative text and other related content.

That means translation does not have to be treated as a completely separate process outside the CMS.

An editor can use AI to create a translation, review it and keep the resulting version as part of the normal Drupal content workflow.

Again, AI is not replacing Drupal's translation system.

It is helping with one of the labour-intensive tasks that happens around that system.

But What About Understanding Documents?

This is where it is important not to exaggerate what Drupal's current AI ecosystem can do.

There is a big difference between saying that AI can summarise or rewrite text and saying that Drupal can completely understand every document uploaded to a website.

Real-world content can include PDFs, scanned documents, spreadsheets, reports, presentations, images and other formats. Understanding all of these reliably is a much broader problem.

AI can certainly be used to process unstructured information, but there is no single Drupal AI module that should be presented as a universal solution for understanding every kind of document and automatically turning it into perfectly structured Drupal content.

That distinction matters.

It is tempting to say that AI will simply "understand all your content", but a production CMS needs more than an impressive demonstration. It needs predictable behaviour, permissions, validation, editorial review and a clear understanding of where information came from.

For Drupal, the more realistic direction is to combine AI capabilities with the structured content and governance that Drupal already provides.

AI Does Not Replace Drupal's Strength

This is probably the most important point.

AI does not make Drupal's existing strengths less important. In many cases, those strengths become more valuable.

An AI model can generate an answer, but where did that information come from?

Drupal can provide the underlying content.

An AI system can suggest a category, but who decides whether that category is correct?

Drupal's editorial workflow can provide the human approval process.

AI can generate a translation, but who has permission to publish it?

Drupal can control that.

AI can help find information, but who owns the content being searched?

Drupal can remain the system where that content is stored and managed.

This creates a useful division of responsibility.

"Drupal manages the content and the rules around it. AI helps people work with that content."

That is a much more realistic way to think about Drupal and AI than imagining that AI will replace the CMS.

The CMS Could Become More Conversational

There is another change that may become increasingly important.

For years, using a CMS has meant navigating screens.

Open the content type. Create a node. Fill in fields. Select taxonomy terms. Upload an image. Save a draft. Send it for review. Publish it.

AI introduces another possible interface.

An editor might eventually be able to say:

"Create a draft announcement from this policy document, keep the language suitable for the public, add the relevant category and prepare it for review."

Instead of manually performing every small operation, the person describes the outcome they want.

But there is an important difference between asking AI to perform a task and giving AI unrestricted control of the CMS.

A production CMS needs permissions, validation and accountability. A user should not be able to casually ask an AI system to publish sensitive content simply because the AI understood the instruction.

That is why Drupal's existing architecture remains important.

The future is unlikely to be "AI instead of Drupal."

It is more likely to be AI working on top of Drupal's existing content, structure, permissions and workflows.

So What Does AI Actually Add to Drupal?

The answer becomes much simpler when the two roles are separated.

Drupal traditionally handles the core CMS lifecycle:

Store → Structure → Edit → Approve → Publish → Deliver

AI adds assistance around the human work involved in that lifecycle:

Create → Rewrite → Summarise → Classify → Search → Translate

Those are not competing responsibilities.

They complement each other.

Drupal is good at being the system that knows what content exists, how that content is structured, who can change it and where it should be delivered.

AI is good at working with language, identifying patterns, generating suggestions and helping people interact with large amounts of information.

Put those together and the CMS becomes more useful without losing the controls that make a CMS dependable.

Drupal's AI Future Is Not About Adding a Chatbot

It is easy to look at AI in a CMS and immediately think about a chatbot.

A chatbot is certainly one possible application, but it is not the most important idea.

The bigger opportunity is making the entire content lifecycle easier.

An editor can get help writing an article. Existing content can be summarised or improved. Taxonomy suggestions can reduce repetitive classification work. Translation can become faster. Search can move from matching keywords towards understanding intent.

None of those require Drupal to stop being Drupal.

In fact, they work precisely because Drupal already provides the underlying content infrastructure.

That may be the most interesting part of the Drupal-and-AI relationship.

AI does not have to replace the CMS to transform how people use it.

It only has to remove enough of the repetitive work around the CMS to let people spend more time deciding what the content should actually say.

And that is probably the better question to ask when evaluating AI for Drupal.

Not "Can AI replace Drupal?"

But:

Which parts of managing content still require too much human effort, and can AI safely reduce that effort?

That is where AI starts making practical sense in Drupal.
