How to Give Sexual Feedback Without Hurting Your Partner's Feelings

By Karthik Kumar D K | Published: Oct 8, 2026 8:28 AM IST
By Karthik Kumar D K , 8 October 2026
How to Give Sexual Feedback Without Hurting Your Partner's Feelings

Talking about sex with your partner can feel surprisingly difficult. You may love each other, trust each other and be comfortable discussing almost everything else, yet saying, “I would like this to be different in bed” can suddenly feel much harder.

The problem is often not the feedback itself. It is how the feedback is delivered.

When sexual feedback sounds like criticism, a partner may feel rejected, embarrassed or inadequate. But when it is presented as a conversation about mutual pleasure and connection, it can actually bring couples closer.

Healthy sexual communication is not about telling your partner everything they are doing wrong. It is about helping each other understand what feels good, what does not, and what you would like to experience together. Research on couples has found that sexual communication is positively associated with both sexual and relationship satisfaction, with the quality of communication particularly important.

Why Giving Sexual Feedback Can Feel So Awkward

Sex is personal. Even a small comment about someone's behaviour can sometimes feel like a comment about them as a person.

For example, saying, “You never understand what I want,” can sound like an attack on your partner.

Saying, “I think I would enjoy it more if we tried this,” creates a completely different conversation.

This distinction matters because your partner may not automatically know what you like. Even couples who have been together for years cannot assume that they know everything about each other's preferences.

Your likes can change. Your comfort level can change. Stress, age, health, pregnancy, parenthood, medication, relationship circumstances and simply getting to know your own body better can all influence what feels right.

So sexual feedback should not be treated as a performance review. It is an ongoing conversation between two people.

Start With What You Appreciate

One of the easiest ways to make sexual feedback feel less threatening is to begin with something genuine that you appreciate.

That does not mean giving your partner compliments just to soften criticism. Be honest.

You might say:

“I really like how close we feel when we take our time.”

“I love when you pay attention to how I'm responding.”

“I feel really comfortable with you, and I want us to keep exploring what works for both of us.”

Starting from appreciation reminds both partners that the conversation is about improving something that already matters to you, rather than fixing a person.

Talk About Your Experience, Not Their Failure

This is probably the most important rule when giving sexual feedback.

Instead of saying:

“You don't know what you're doing.”

Try:

“I think I would enjoy it more if we slowed down.”

Instead of:

“You never listen to me.”

Try:

“I'd like to tell you more about what I enjoy.”

Instead of:

“That doesn't feel good.”

Try:

“Could we try something a little different?”

The difference is subtle but powerful.

When you describe your experience and your preferences, your partner has something useful to respond to. When you make a judgment about their ability, they may become defensive instead.

Good sexual communication is less about assigning blame and more about giving each other information.

Be Specific About What You Want

“Do it better” is not useful feedback.

Neither is “I don't know, just do something different.”

If you want something to change, try to explain what you would prefer.

You could talk about pace, communication, affection, timing, atmosphere or simply spending more time connecting before becoming intimate.

You do not need to turn the conversation into a detailed instruction manual. Even a simple sentence can help:

“I like it when we don't rush.”

“I'd like you to check in with me more.”

“I feel more relaxed when we have some privacy and don't feel rushed.”

“I'd like us to talk more about what we both enjoy.”

The clearer you are, the easier it becomes for your partner to understand what you actually mean.

Choose the Right Time

A sexual conversation does not always need to happen in the middle of intimacy.

If something has been bothering you for a while, bringing it up during an emotionally charged moment may make it harder for both of you.

Sometimes a relaxed conversation outside the bedroom is better.

You might say, “Can I talk to you about something I'd like us to try differently?”

That simple opening gives your partner an opportunity to listen rather than suddenly feeling attacked.

There is also nothing wrong with communicating during intimacy. A gentle “I like that” or “could you stay there?” can be much easier than waiting until later to explain everything.

Research has also found that both verbal and non-verbal sexual communication can be relevant to sexual satisfaction.

Don't Compare Your Partner to Someone Else

Comparisons can turn a useful conversation into a painful one very quickly.

Avoid statements such as:

“My ex used to…”

“Other couples…”

“You should be more like…”

Your partner is not competing with another person.

Sexual compatibility develops between two specific people. What worked in another relationship may not even be what you want now.

Instead of bringing someone else into the conversation, focus on the relationship you are building together.

Give Positive Feedback Too

Sexual communication should not happen only when something is wrong.

If something feels good, say so.

A simple “I really liked that” can be valuable feedback.

Positive reinforcement helps your partner understand what you enjoy without making every conversation about correcting something.

It also makes future conversations easier because both partners become more comfortable talking openly about sex.

Listen to Their Feedback Too

Giving sexual feedback is only half of the conversation.

Your partner may have preferences, concerns or suggestions of their own.

Try not to treat their feedback as criticism either.

You might discover that something you assumed your partner disliked is actually something they enjoy. Or you may learn that they have been hesitant to ask for something because they were worried about how you would react.

A healthy sex life requires space for both people to speak.

The goal is not for one person to become the teacher and the other the student. It is for both partners to learn about each other.

What If Your Partner Gets Hurt?

Even when you choose your words carefully, your partner may still feel sensitive about the subject.

If that happens, don't immediately argue about whether they should feel hurt.

Try acknowledging the feeling.

You could say:

“I didn't mean that as a criticism of you.”

“I can understand why that sounded difficult.”

“I love being close to you. I'm telling you this because I want us to understand each other better.”

Sometimes the emotional reaction needs attention before the actual sexual issue can be discussed.

And remember that your partner is allowed to have feelings about your feedback. Respectful communication does not guarantee that every conversation will be comfortable.

Sexual Feedback Should Be a Two-Way Conversation

The healthiest approach is to think of sexual feedback as an ongoing conversation rather than a one-time discussion.

People change. Relationships change. Sexual preferences can change too.

What worked when you first started dating may not be exactly what works years later.

That does not necessarily mean something is wrong with the relationship.

It may simply mean that you have more to learn about each other.

Couples who communicate openly about sex are not necessarily couples who agree about everything. They are couples who can discuss differences without turning those differences into personal attacks. Studies have found meaningful links between sexual communication and both sexual and relationship satisfaction.

The Bottom Line

Giving sexual feedback does not have to mean telling your partner what they are doing wrong.

Think of it as saying, “Here is what I enjoy, here is what I would like to try, and here is how we can make this better for both of us.”

Be specific without being harsh. Talk about your experience rather than attacking your partner. Choose a comfortable time. Appreciate what already works. And most importantly, give your partner the same freedom to communicate their needs.

Good sexual communication is not about achieving perfect performance.

It is about feeling comfortable enough with each other to say what you need, listen without judgment and keep learning together.

That kind of conversation can improve more than your sex life. It can strengthen trust, intimacy and the feeling that both partners are genuinely being heard.
