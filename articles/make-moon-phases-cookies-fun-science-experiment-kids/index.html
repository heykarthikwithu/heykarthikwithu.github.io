Make the Moon Phases With Cookies: A Fun Science Experiment for Kids

By Karthik Kumar D K | Published: Sep 8, 2026 8:53 AM IST
By Karthik Kumar D K , 8 September 2026
Make the Moon Phases With Cookies: A Fun Science Experiment for Kids

Look up at the Moon tonight. What shape do you see?

Maybe it looks like a big, bright circle. Maybe it looks like a banana-shaped crescent. Or perhaps you can only see half of it.

But here is a cool question: Does the Moon actually change shape?

Nope!

The Moon is always a round ball. What changes is how much of the Moon's sunny side we can see from Earth.

In this fun Moon phases science experiment, you can use cookies to make the eight different phases of the Moon. It's a tasty way to learn about space, and you don't need a telescope or fancy science equipment.

So grab some cookies and let's explore the Moon!

What Are Moon Phases?

The Moon doesn't make its own light. The Sun does that job.

Think of the Moon as a giant ball floating in space. The Sun shines on one side of the ball. As the Moon travels around Earth, we see different amounts of its bright, sunlit side.

That's why the Moon seems to change shape during the month.

These different shapes are called the phases of the Moon.

There are eight main Moon phases:

  1. New Moon
  2. Waxing Crescent
  3. First Quarter
  4. Waxing Gibbous
  5. Full Moon
  6. Waning Gibbous
  7. Third Quarter
  8. Waning Crescent

After the Waning Crescent, the Moon starts the whole cycle again.

The Moon takes about 29.5 days to go through one complete cycle of phases.

Let's Make the Moon With Cookies!

Now it's time to become a Moon scientist.

For this activity, the cookie cream represents the part of the Moon lit by the Sun. The dark part of the cookie represents the part that isn't lit up from our point of view.

You aren't actually changing the Moon. You're making a model that shows what the Moon looks like from Earth.

What You Need?

Here's your simple science equipment:

  • 8 round sandwich cookies with cream filling
  • A plate or piece of paper
  • A spoon or butter knife
  • A marker
  • Small pieces of paper for labels

It's a good idea to have a few extra cookies nearby. Cookie science can be a little messy!

Ask an adult to help if you need to use a knife or another sharp tool.

Step 1: Make the New Moon

Let's start with the New Moon.

Carefully twist a cookie apart and remove almost all of the cream.

Your cookie should look mostly dark.

Why?

During a New Moon, the side of the Moon facing Earth is not getting much sunlight that we can see. The Moon is still up there, but it is very difficult to see.

Put your cookie on the plate and write New Moon underneath it.

Science fact: A New Moon doesn't mean the Moon has disappeared. It's still there!

Step 2: Make a Waxing Crescent

Now make the Waxing Crescent.

Leave just a small curved piece of cream showing on one side of the cookie.

This looks like a tiny slice of a Moon.

The word waxing means that the bright part we can see is getting bigger.

So during the waxing phases, the Moon seems to be growing from our point of view.

Label your cookie Waxing Crescent.

Step 3: Make the First Quarter

Next comes the First Quarter.

Leave about half of the cream showing.

You might think this should be called the "Half Moon," and you're not wrong—it certainly looks like half a circle!

But scientists call it the First Quarter because the Moon is about one-quarter of the way through its trip around Earth.

Label your cookie First Quarter.

Step 4: Make a Waxing Gibbous

Now we're getting closer to a Full Moon.

For the Waxing Gibbous, leave more than half of the cream showing.

It should look like a big, almost-full Moon.

The Moon is still waxing, so the bright part we see is continuing to grow.

Gibbous is a funny word, but it has a simple meaning. It describes a Moon that looks more than half full but isn't completely full.

Label your cookie Waxing Gibbous.

Step 5: Make the Full Moon

Now comes the big one: the Full Moon!

For this cookie, leave all of the cream showing.

The Full Moon looks like a bright, round circle in the night sky.

This happens because the side of the Moon facing Earth is fully lit by the Sun.

Look at your cookie.

It may look like a little Moon sitting on your plate!

Label it Full Moon.

Step 6: Make a Waning Gibbous

After the Full Moon, something changes.

The bright part we see starts getting smaller.

This is called waning.

For the Waning Gibbous, remove some of the cream so that more than half is still showing.

It should look similar to the Waxing Gibbous—but the bright part is on the other side.

Label it Waning Gibbous.

Step 7: Make the Third Quarter

Next is the Third Quarter.

Leave about half of the cream showing again.

It looks like another Half Moon, but the bright side is opposite the First Quarter.

Here's a fun way to remember it:

First Quarter = half Moon while waxing

Third Quarter = half Moon while waning

Label your cookie Third Quarter.

Step 8: Make the Waning Crescent

We're almost finished!

For the Waning Crescent, leave just a small curved piece of cream showing.

This is the last phase before the Moon becomes a New Moon again.

The bright part is now very small.

Label your cookie Waning Crescent.

And that's it! You've made all eight phases of the Moon.

Put Your Moon Phases in Order

Now arrange your cookies in the correct order.

Start with:

New Moon → Waxing Crescent → First Quarter → Waxing Gibbous → Full Moon → Waning Gibbous → Third Quarter → Waning Crescent

Then the cycle starts again with the New Moon.

Take a look at your cookie Moon.

Can you see a pattern?

Starting at the New Moon, the bright part gets bigger and bigger until the Full Moon.

Then it gets smaller and smaller until the next New Moon.

That's the Moon's cycle!

But Why Does the Moon Change?

Here's the most important part of this Moon phases activity.

The Moon isn't changing shape.

The Moon is always round.

Instead, the Moon is moving around Earth. The Sun is shining on the Moon the entire time, but we see different amounts of the lit-up side as the Moon moves.

Imagine holding a ball near a lamp in a dark room.

The lamp lights up one side of the ball. If you walk around the ball, the bright part you see changes.

That's basically what happens with the Moon!

The Sun is the light source, the Moon is the ball, and Earth is where we are watching from.

Can You Remember Waxing and Waning?

These two words can sound tricky, but there's an easy way to remember them.

Waxing = getting bigger

The bright part of the Moon we see is growing.

Waning = getting smaller

The bright part of the Moon we see is shrinking.

Try saying it out loud:

Waxing grows. Waning shrinks.

Now you know one of the most important ideas about Moon phases!

Try This Moon Challenge

Want to become a real Moon detective?

For the next few weeks, look at the Moon whenever you can.

Draw what you see in a notebook.

Write down:

  • The date
  • What the Moon looks like
  • Whether it is waxing or waning
  • Which Moon phase you think it is

Don't worry if your drawing isn't perfect. You're doing science, not entering an art contest!

After a few weeks, look back at your drawings.

Can you see the Moon changing from a thin crescent to a larger Moon and then back again?

What Did You Discover?

You started with some cookies, but you discovered something much bigger.

You learned that:

  • The Moon is always round.
  • The Moon does not make its own light.
  • The Sun lights up the Moon.
  • The Moon travels around Earth.
  • We see different amounts of the Moon's lit-up side.
  • These changes create the eight Moon phases.
  • Waxing means the bright part is growing.
  • Waning means the bright part is getting smaller.
  • One complete Moon phase cycle takes about 29.5 days.

So the next time you look into the night sky and see a tiny crescent or a bright Full Moon, you'll know what's really happening.

The Moon isn't changing shape. It's putting on a changing light show as it travels around Earth!

And now you can explain it using your very own cookie Moon model.
