Seed Dispersal: Explore How Seeds Travel by Wind, Water and Animals

By Karthik Kumar D K | Published: Sep 22, 2026 11:23 AM IST
By Karthik Kumar D K , 22 September 2026
Seed Dispersal: Explore How Seeds Travel by Wind, Water and Animals

Have you ever picked a tiny seed off your clothes after walking through a park or garden and wondered how it got there? Or noticed dry leaves and seeds being blown along the ground on a windy day?

Plants cannot walk around looking for a good place to grow, but their seeds can travel surprisingly far. Depending on the plant, seeds may be carried by wind, float away in water, or get a lift from an animal. Some plants even have fruits or seed pods that help scatter their seeds on their own.

The movement of seeds from one place to another is known as seed dispersal. It is a simple idea, but it plays an important role in helping plants spread and grow.

You can explore this process at home or in the classroom with a few everyday materials. The activity below looks at three common ways seeds travel: wind, water and animals.

What Is Seed Dispersal?

Once a plant produces seeds, those seeds need to find a suitable place to grow. If every seed simply dropped underneath the parent plant, many of them would end up competing for the same sunlight, water, nutrients and space.

Moving away from the parent plant gives seeds a better chance of finding a suitable place to germinate.

Different plants have different ways of doing this. Some seeds are light enough to be carried by the wind. Others can float on water. Some have tiny hooks or rough surfaces that allow them to cling to animal fur.

For this experiment, we are going to see how these different features can help seeds travel.

What You Will Need

You don't need any special science equipment. Gather:

  • A few different types of seeds
  • A dry leaf
  • A few small pieces of paper
  • A bowl or shallow container of water
  • A piece of cotton or wool fabric
  • A spoon
  • A notebook and pencil

You can use seeds that are easy to find at home, such as beans, mustard seeds, coriander seeds, sunflower seeds or rice grains. If you have access to seeds from plants growing outdoors, you can include those too.

Avoid eating any seeds after using them for the experiment, particularly seeds collected from outdoors.

Experiment 1: Can Wind Carry a Seed?

Walk outside on a windy day and you will quickly notice how easily light objects move around. A dry leaf can travel across the ground, while a heavier stone hardly moves at all.

Some plants take advantage of the same basic idea.

Dandelion seeds, for example, have a fluffy structure that acts somewhat like a tiny parachute. Some winged seeds, such as those produced by maple trees, spin as they fall and can be carried away from the parent plant.

Try It Yourself

Take a dry leaf, a small piece of paper and one of your seeds.

Hold each object at roughly the same height and drop them one at a time.

Watch what happens.

Does one object reach the ground faster than the others? Does anything drift sideways? Does the shape of the object seem to make a difference?

Now take your piece of paper and drop it once while it is flat. Crumple it into a small ball and drop it again from the same height.

The flat paper will usually take longer to fall because it has a larger surface area pushing against the air.

What Did You Notice?

This gives us an idea of why many wind-dispersed seeds are either very light or have special shapes that help them stay in the air.

A seed doesn't need to fly like a bird. Even staying in the air for a little longer can give the wind enough time to carry it away from the parent plant.

Experiment 2: Can Seeds Travel by Water?

Now let's move from air to water.

Think about plants that grow near rivers, ponds or the sea. Their seeds and fruits may fall into the water and be carried somewhere else.

The coconut is a familiar example. Its thick, fibrous outer covering helps it float, which means water can carry it over considerable distances.

You can test the basic idea with your own collection of seeds.

Try It Yourself

Fill a shallow bowl with water.

Place your different seeds into the water one at a time. Don't push them under; simply place each one on the surface and watch what happens.

Make a note of whether each seed:

  • Floats
  • Sinks straight away
  • Floats for a while and then sinks
  • Moves when you create a gentle current with a spoon

Leave the seeds that float in the water for several minutes and check them again.

What Did You Notice?

Not every seed is designed to float, and that is perfectly fine. Plants that depend on water for dispersal often have features that help their seeds or fruits stay afloat.

A lightweight structure, air spaces or a fibrous outer covering can make a difference.

If a seed falls into a stream or river, the moving water can carry it away from the plant that produced it. If it eventually reaches a suitable place, it may germinate and grow into a new plant.

Experiment 3: Can Animals Carry Seeds?

This is probably one of the easiest types of seed dispersal to spot.

Have you ever come home from a walk and found little prickly seeds stuck to your socks or clothes? Those seeds have found a clever way to travel without having to move themselves.

Some seeds have hooks, spines or rough surfaces that catch onto animal fur. As the animal walks around, the seeds travel with it. Eventually, they may fall off somewhere else.

Animals can also spread seeds in another way. Birds and other animals eat fruits, and the seeds inside those fruits can later be deposited in a different location.

Try It Yourself

Take a small piece of cotton or wool fabric. Think of it as a simple model of animal fur.

Press different seeds against the fabric and move them around gently.

Which ones stay attached?

Now compare a smooth seed with a rough seed. If you have a seed with a tiny hook or spiky surface, try that one too.

Ask yourself why some seeds are easier to pick up than others.

What Did You Notice?

A seed with a rough or hooked surface has a better chance of catching onto fur than a smooth seed.

This is known as external animal dispersal because the seed travels on the outside of the animal.

There is also internal animal dispersal, which happens when an animal eats a fruit containing seeds. The animal later deposits those seeds somewhere else. In this way, the plant gets some help from the animal in spreading its next generation.

What Did the Experiment Show?

Once you've finished the three activities, compare your observations.

Dispersal MethodSeed or Fruit FeatureHow It Travels
WindLightweight, wings or fluffy structuresCarried by moving air
WaterBuoyant or fibrous coveringFloats and moves with water
AnimalsHooks, spines or rough surfacesSticks to fur or is carried after being eaten

The interesting part is that there isn't one "best" seed design. A feature that is useful for one type of dispersal may not be useful for another.

A fluffy seed is good at catching the wind. A floating fruit needs a different structure. A seed that travels on an animal needs something that can catch onto fur.

Why Do Plants Need to Disperse Their Seeds?

Seed dispersal helps plants spread into new areas. It also reduces competition between a young plant and the parent plant growing nearby.

Imagine a tree dropping hundreds of seeds directly underneath itself. The seedlings would all be competing for the same water, nutrients, sunlight and space. If some of those seeds are carried away, they have a chance to grow somewhere else.

Seed dispersal also helps plants reach areas where they have not grown before. Over time, wind, water and animals can help spread plants across gardens, forests, grasslands and other habitats.

In fact, seed dispersal is happening around us all the time. We simply don't always notice it.

Try This Extra Challenge

Once you've finished the experiment, choose five different seeds and try to work out how each one might travel in nature.

Before looking up the answer, examine each seed carefully.

Ask yourself:

  • Is it light or heavy?
  • Is it smooth, rough or sticky?
  • Does it have wings, hairs or hooks?
  • Can it float?
  • Does it look like something an animal could carry?

Write down your prediction for each seed.

Then find out which plant it comes from and how its seeds are actually dispersed. Were your guesses correct?

This makes the activity more than just a simple observation. You're making a prediction, looking at evidence and then checking your idea — exactly the kind of thinking scientists use.

Conclusion

Seeds may look small and simple, but many of them have interesting ways of getting around. Some catch the wind, some float with the water, and others use animals as their transportation.

The next time you find a seed stuck to your shoe or watch one being blown across the ground, take a closer look. There may be a clever little bit of plant science behind it.

This seed dispersal experiment is a simple way to see how the shape and structure of a seed can determine where it goes. And once you start looking, you'll probably notice examples of seed dispersal almost everywhere.
