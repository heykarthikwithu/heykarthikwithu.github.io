Living Beyond Your Means: Can Debt, Status and Social Pressure Lead to Crime?

By Karthik Kumar D K | Published: Oct 28, 2026 9:15 AM IST
By Karthik Kumar D K , 28 October 2026
Living Beyond Your Means: Can Debt, Status and Social Pressure Lead to Crime?

A good lifestyle is something many people work hard to achieve. A comfortable home, a reliable car, fashionable clothes, family holidays and the ability to enjoy life's pleasures are common aspirations. There is nothing wrong with wanting these things. The problem begins when maintaining an image of success becomes more important than the financial reality behind it.

In today's world, success is often judged by what people own, where they travel, what they wear and the lifestyle they display on social media. A person's financial struggles may remain hidden behind expensive purchases, restaurant photographs and carefully presented images of happiness.

For some, the gap between what they earn and how they want to live creates a cycle of borrowing, anxiety and financial pressure. Most people facing such difficulties try to adjust their spending, find additional income or gradually repay their debts. However, in some circumstances, the desire to maintain an unaffordable lifestyle can contribute to dishonest decisions, financial fraud and other forms of crime.

This raises an important question: Can the pressure to appear successful push people towards crime?

When Lifestyle Becomes a Financial Burden

Living beyond one's means does not necessarily mean being wealthy or poor. It means spending more than one's income can comfortably support, often by relying on credit, loans or money that should have been saved for future needs.

Consider someone who earns a reasonable monthly salary but wants to maintain the lifestyle of a person earning several times more. A new smartphone is followed by branded clothing, restaurant visits, expensive celebrations and frequent online shopping. Each purchase may appear manageable on its own, but the combined expenses can gradually become difficult to sustain.

Credit cards and instant personal loans can make this lifestyle seem affordable. Instead of paying the full cost immediately, a person can spread expenses across monthly instalments. The immediate financial burden appears smaller, even when the total cost becomes much higher.

The difficulty emerges when existing instalments consume a growing share of monthly income. Rent, household expenses, education costs and unexpected emergencies still need to be paid. Another loan may temporarily solve the problem, but it can also create a larger obligation for the following month.

The Reserve Bank of India has highlighted concerns surrounding excessive borrowing for consumption and lifestyle needs. Its work on digital lending has also drawn attention to the risks associated with easy access to credit, inadequate assessment of repayment capacity and irresponsible lending practices.

Debt itself is not a crime. Borrowing to purchase a home, fund education or manage an emergency can be a sensible financial decision. The danger arises when borrowing becomes a way to sustain spending that income can no longer support.

The Pressure to Look Successful

Financial decisions are not always based on practical needs. They can also be influenced by the desire to belong, gain respect or avoid embarrassment.

Imagine a young professional whose colleagues frequently discuss international holidays, new cars and expensive restaurants. The professional may begin to feel that an ordinary lifestyle makes them appear less successful, even if their finances are healthier than those of some people in the group.

Similar pressure can arise during weddings, family celebrations, school reunions and social gatherings. People may spend beyond their capacity because they fear criticism, comparison or the disappointment of relatives.

Social media can intensify this pressure. People usually share the highlights of their lives rather than their unpaid bills, financial worries or disagreements over money. Someone viewing these posts may compare their everyday reality with another person's carefully selected moments.

The result can be a distorted understanding of success. A person may begin to believe that happiness and social acceptance depend on displaying expensive possessions.

There is a crucial difference between wanting a better life and feeling compelled to prove that one has a better life.

When appearances become a priority, financial decisions may be made to protect an image rather than meet genuine needs. That pressure can become especially difficult when a person has already accumulated debt and feels unable to admit the problem.

How Financial Pressure Can Lead to Crime

Financial stress does not automatically turn someone into a criminal. Millions of people experience debt, unemployment and economic hardship without engaging in illegal activities. The vast majority look for legitimate ways to manage their difficulties.

Nevertheless, financial pressure can create circumstances in which some individuals begin to rationalise dishonest behaviour.

1. Fraud to Maintain an Expensive Lifestyle

A person struggling to pay existing debts may be tempted to obtain money through false promises, fabricated investment opportunities or misleading business proposals. They might persuade friends, relatives or acquaintances to invest by promising unrealistic returns.

Initially, the money may be used to settle overdue payments or cover personal expenses. If the person then uses money from new investors to repay earlier investors, the situation can develop into a fraudulent scheme.

The underlying problem is no longer simply a lack of money. It becomes a decision to mislead other people to preserve a lifestyle or conceal financial losses.

2. Misuse of Money Entrusted by Others

Financial pressure can also create temptation in workplaces and businesses. Someone who handles company funds, customer payments or financial records may consider using money temporarily, believing they can replace it before anyone notices.

That reasoning can be dangerous. Once money is misused, the individual may conceal the original act, falsify records or create further transactions to cover the shortage.

Depending on the facts and applicable law, such conduct may amount to misappropriation, cheating, breach of trust or other offences.

3. Illegal Borrowing and Deception

When legitimate lenders refuse further credit, a financially distressed person may turn to questionable arrangements or misrepresent their income, liabilities or ability to repay.

Not every inaccurate statement has the same legal consequences, and genuine inability to repay a loan is not automatically criminal. However, deliberate deception used to obtain money can cross the line into fraud.

There is also a separate danger for borrowers who turn to unauthorised loan applications or predatory lenders. Some face excessive charges, misuse of personal information or intimidating recovery practices. People under financial pressure should verify a lender's legitimacy before sharing documents or accepting a loan.

4. Gambling and the Pursuit of Quick Money

Someone who wants to recover financial losses quickly may turn to gambling or speculative schemes. After losing money, they may continue taking risks because they believe the next attempt will solve everything.

For a person already struggling with debt, this can make the situation worse. If the losses become difficult to conceal, the temptation to borrow again, deceive family members or obtain money dishonestly may increase.

None of these outcomes is inevitable. They illustrate how the combination of financial pressure, unrealistic expectations and repeated attempts to avoid consequences can create a dangerous pattern.

Why Hiding Financial Problems Can Make Things Worse

One of the most damaging aspects of lifestyle-driven debt is the reluctance to discuss it openly.

A person may continue buying expensive gifts while quietly worrying about loan repayments. They may tell family members that everything is under control, avoid calls from lenders or conceal the amount owed on credit cards.

The longer the problem remains hidden, the harder it may become to address.

Shame can prevent people from seeking help early. Fear of losing respect may encourage them to borrow more money rather than acknowledge that their spending needs to change. In extreme cases, protecting their reputation becomes more important than protecting their financial future.

This is where the psychological burden of debt can become significant. Financial stress may affect sleep, relationships, concentration and decision-making. A person who feels trapped may focus on immediate relief rather than the longer-term consequences of their choices.

Recognising this pressure is important, but it does not excuse harming others. A person facing genuine financial hardship deserves support. A person who deliberately deceives others must also be held accountable for that conduct.

Both realities can exist at the same time.

Are We Judging Success by the Wrong Standards?

The wider social environment deserves attention too.

Families, workplaces and communities often celebrate visible achievements: buying a car, purchasing a property, hosting a lavish wedding or wearing expensive brands. These milestones can represent years of effort and deserve recognition when achieved responsibly.

However, when society treats spending as proof of success, it can overlook more meaningful signs of financial stability.

A person who lives in a modest home, avoids unnecessary debt and maintains emergency savings may be in a stronger financial position than someone who owns expensive possessions but struggles to pay monthly bills.

Likewise, someone who postpones a purchase until they can afford it is not necessarily less ambitious. They may simply be making a more sustainable decision.

Changing these attitudes does not mean discouraging ambition or comfortable living. It means recognising that financial independence, honesty and peace of mind are also measures of success.

How to Avoid the Debt and Status Trap

Preventing lifestyle-driven financial trouble starts with a few practical habits.

Know what you can afford. Track income, essential expenses, existing loan repayments and discretionary spending. Make purchasing decisions based on actual affordability rather than appearances.

Understand the full cost of borrowing. Before taking a personal loan or using a credit card, check the interest rate, fees, repayment schedule and consequences of missed payments. Small instalments can hide a substantial overall cost.

Learn to say no to social pressure. You do not need to match another person's spending to maintain a friendship or earn respect. Set a budget for celebrations, travel and shopping, and stick to it.

Address debt early. If repayments are becoming difficult, contact your lender to discuss legitimate options. Avoid taking another expensive loan without understanding whether it will improve or worsen your situation. A qualified financial counsellor may also help you assess your options.

Never use dishonesty as a financial solution. Do not falsify documents, misuse entrusted money or mislead others into investments. Such actions can create legal consequences and transfer your financial burden to innocent people.

Most importantly, be willing to discuss financial difficulties with someone trustworthy before they become unmanageable.

The Real Cost of Living Beyond Your Means

The cost of an unaffordable lifestyle is not limited to interest payments or credit-card bills. It can include strained relationships, lost trust, anxiety and years spent recovering from financial decisions made under pressure.

For some people, the consequences remain financial. Others may make dishonest choices that cause serious harm to colleagues, friends, customers or family members.

The lesson is not that people who borrow money are irresponsible or that everyone who enjoys luxury is living beyond their means. It is that the desire to maintain an image can become dangerous when it overrides financial reality and personal responsibility.

A comfortable lifestyle should improve the quality of life, not become a burden that must be defended at any cost.

Ultimately, real success is not about convincing others that you can afford everything. It is about building a life you can genuinely afford, without sacrificing your peace of mind, your integrity or the trust of the people around you.
