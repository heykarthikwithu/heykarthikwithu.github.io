Density Tower with Kitchen Ingredients: A Fun Science Experiment for Kids

By Karthik Kumar D K | Published: Nov 10, 2026 1:47 PM IST
By Karthik Kumar D K , 10 November 2026
Density Tower with Kitchen Ingredients: A Fun Science Experiment for Kids

Have you ever wondered why some liquids float on top of others instead of mixing together? You can discover the answer with a colourful science experiment using ingredients from your kitchen!

The Density Tower experiment is a simple and exciting activity in which children layer different liquids inside a transparent glass or jar. Instead of mixing into one another, the liquids form separate layers, creating a colourful tower. The interesting part is that each liquid has a different density, which determines where it settles in the container.

You do not need special laboratory equipment to try this experiment. A few everyday kitchen ingredients, a clear glass and some patience are enough to explore the science of density.

Let's make a colourful density tower and find out why some liquids float while others sink!

What Is the Density Tower Experiment?

A density tower is a science experiment that demonstrates how liquids with different densities behave when they are carefully poured into the same container.

Density tells us how much mass is packed into a particular volume of a substance. A liquid with greater density generally settles below a liquid with lower density when the two are placed together.

For example, honey is denser than cooking oil. When both are poured into a transparent container carefully, honey settles at the bottom while oil floats above it.

By using several kitchen ingredients, children can create multiple visible layers and observe how liquids arrange themselves according to their density.

Materials Required for the Density Tower Experiment

You can find most of these materials in your kitchen.

  • A tall, transparent glass or clear plastic container
  • Honey
  • Dishwashing liquid
  • Water
  • Cooking oil
  • Rubbing alcohol (optional, adult handling only)
  • Food colouring (optional)
  • A spoon
  • A small measuring cup
  • A dropper or spoon for careful pouring

Tip: You do not have to use every ingredient. Honey, dishwashing liquid, coloured water and cooking oil are enough for a simple density tower.

Step-by-Step Instructions: How to Make a Density Tower

Follow these instructions carefully to create your own colourful liquid tower.

Step 1: Prepare Your Container

Take a clean, tall and transparent glass or jar. Make sure it is completely dry before you begin.

A transparent container is important because it allows you to see the different liquid layers clearly.

Step 2: Add Honey

Pour a small amount of honey into the bottom of the container.

Honey is relatively dense, so it will form the bottom layer of your density tower.

Allow it to settle before adding the next ingredient.

Step 3: Add Dishwashing Liquid

Slowly pour dishwashing liquid over the honey.

To avoid disturbing the first layer, pour the liquid gently down the inside wall of the glass or over the back of a spoon.

You should begin to see two separate layers.

Step 4: Add Coloured Water

Take some water and add a few drops of food colouring. Blue or green food colouring makes the experiment especially interesting.

Slowly add the coloured water to the container.

Pouring gently helps prevent the liquids from mixing too much.

Step 5: Add Cooking Oil

Now carefully pour cooking oil into the container.

You should notice that the oil forms a layer above the water because oil is less dense than water and does not mix with it.

Step 6: Observe Your Density Tower

Look at the different layers inside the glass.

You should see honey near the bottom, followed by dishwashing liquid, water and cooking oil near the top.

The exact arrangement can vary depending on the brands and formulations of the ingredients, so observe what happens rather than assuming every ingredient will always form a perfectly separate layer.

Your colourful density tower is ready!

The Science Behind the Density Tower

Why do the liquids form separate layers instead of mixing together?

The answer is density.

Density is the amount of mass contained in a given volume of a substance. In simple words, it tells us how much matter is packed into a particular space.

A liquid with higher density generally settles below a liquid with lower density when they are placed together and allowed to settle.

For example:

  • Honey is generally denser than water.
  • Water is denser than most cooking oils.
  • Cooking oil usually floats above water because it has lower density.

This is why the ingredients can form different layers inside the same glass.

However, density is not the only factor involved. Some liquids mix with each other, while others do not. For example, water and cooking oil separate because they are immiscible, meaning they do not mix into a single uniform liquid.

The density tower therefore demonstrates two interesting scientific ideas: density and the ability of liquids to mix.

What Did We Observe?

After completing the experiment, children can record their observations.

IngredientExpected position
HoneyBottom
Dishwashing liquidUsually below water
Coloured waterAbove honey and often above dishwashing liquid
Cooking oilTop

These are typical results, not guaranteed positions for every brand. Dishwashing liquids have different formulations and densities, so their position may change.

The most important observation is that liquids with different densities can settle into layers, provided they do not mix significantly.

Why Does Cooking Oil Float on Water?

This is one of the easiest ways to understand density.

Water has a density of approximately 1 gram per millilitre at room temperature. Most common cooking oils have a lower density, usually around 0.91–0.93 grams per millilitre.

Since cooking oil is less dense than water, it floats on top.

Another interesting fact is that oil and water do not mix easily. Even if you shake them together, they eventually separate into two layers when left undisturbed.

Fun Challenges for Kids

Once you have created your first density tower, try these additional activities.

1. Change the Colours

Use different food colours in separate experiments. Observe whether the colour makes it easier to identify each layer.

2. Try Different Cooking Oils

Compare sunflower oil, groundnut oil and coconut oil. Do they all float on water? What differences can you observe?

3. Add Small Objects

With adult supervision, gently place a small, clean object into the tower. Observe whether it sinks or floats at different levels. Its position depends on its average density and shape.

4. Predict Before Pouring

Before adding a new ingredient, ask children to predict whether it will settle at the top, middle or bottom. Then perform the experiment and compare the results.

Questions to Ask Children

Make the experiment more educational by discussing these questions:

  1. Why does honey settle at the bottom?
  2. Why does cooking oil float on water?
  3. Do all liquids have the same density?
  4. What happens when you shake oil and water together?
  5. Can you create a density tower using other household liquids?

Encourage children to explain their observations in their own words. This helps them connect what they see with what they learn about science.

Safety Precautions

Although this is a simple kitchen science experiment, children should follow a few safety rules.

  • Ask an adult to help with the experiment.
  • Do not drink or taste any of the liquids after mixing them.
  • Never use rubbing alcohol or other cleaning chemicals unless an adult is supervising and has approved the activity.
  • Keep the experiment away from flames and hot surfaces.
  • Clean up spilled oil immediately because it can make floors slippery.
  • Wash your hands after completing the experiment.

Important: If you use rubbing alcohol for an advanced version, an adult must handle it. It is flammable and must never be placed near flames or heat.

What Have We Learned?

The Density Tower experiment shows that liquids are not all the same. They have different densities, and this affects how they behave when placed together.

Honey, dishwashing liquid, coloured water and cooking oil can create a fascinating layered arrangement. By observing these layers, children can understand why some liquids settle at the bottom while others remain on top.

The best part is that this experiment does not require expensive laboratory equipment. A few kitchen ingredients and a transparent glass are enough to turn an ordinary afternoon into an exciting science activity.

So, gather your ingredients, make a colourful density tower and discover the science hiding in your kitchen!
