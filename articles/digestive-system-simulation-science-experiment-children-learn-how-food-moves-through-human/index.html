Digestive System Simulation: Science Experiment for Children to Learn How Food Moves Through the Human Body

By Karthik Kumar D K | Published: Aug 10, 2026 8:47 AM IST
By Karthik Kumar D K , 10 August 2026
Digestive System Simulation: Science Experiment for Children to Learn How Food Moves Through the Human Body

How does the food we eat travel through our body? What happens to a cracker after we swallow it? How does the body turn a banana or other food into nutrients that we can use for energy?

These are fascinating questions for children, and the digestive system simulation using crackers and bananas provides a simple way to explore the answers. Instead of only reading about the stomach, small intestine, and large intestine in a textbook, children can create a hands-on model that demonstrates some of the basic mechanical processes involved in digestion.

This children's science experiment uses simple household materials and is suitable for a classroom, homeschooling activity, or science project at home. The experiment is not an exact replica of human digestion, but it provides a visual and easy-to-understand model of how food is broken down as it travels through the digestive system.

What Is the Digestive System?

The digestive system is a group of organs that work together to process the food we eat. Its main job is to break food down into smaller substances that the body can absorb and use.

The major parts involved include:

  • Mouth
  • Esophagus
  • Stomach
  • Small intestine
  • Large intestine
  • Rectum
  • Anus

Other organs, including the liver, pancreas, and gallbladder, also help with digestion by producing or storing substances needed to break down food.

Digestion begins even before food reaches the stomach. When we chew food, our teeth physically break it into smaller pieces, while saliva begins the chemical digestion of certain nutrients.

How Does This Experiment Work?

The cracker and banana experiment creates a simplified model of the digestive process.

Children can use the materials to represent different stages of digestion:

Mouth → Esophagus → Stomach → Small Intestine → Large Intestine

The cracker represents a food that needs to be broken down, while the banana can be combined with it to demonstrate how different foods can become mixed together during digestion.

The experiment focuses mainly on mechanical breakdown and movement, rather than attempting to reproduce every chemical reaction that takes place inside the human digestive system.

Materials Required

You don't need expensive laboratory equipment for this activity.

You will need:

  • 2–3 plain crackers
  • ½ banana
  • A small transparent plastic bag
  • A small amount of water
  • A bowl or container
  • A spoon
  • A pair of scissors
  • A transparent flexible tube or clean zip-top bag for demonstration
  • Paper towels
  • A tray to protect the working surface

For younger children, an adult should supervise the activity, particularly when scissors or small objects are being used.

Step 1: Start With the Mouth

Begin by giving children a cracker and asking them to observe it.

Ask:

"What do you think happens to this cracker when you eat it?"

Let the children describe what they know.

Explain that digestion begins in the mouth.

Our teeth break food into smaller pieces through chewing. Saliva also mixes with the food and begins the digestion of carbohydrates.

For the experiment, place a cracker and a small piece of banana into a bowl.

Use a spoon to crush and mix them together.

Explain that this represents some of the physical changes that happen when we chew food.

The food doesn't simply disappear when we chew it. Instead, it becomes softer and broken into smaller pieces.

Step 2: Understanding the Esophagus

Once food has been chewed and swallowed, it travels through the esophagus, a muscular tube connecting the mouth to the stomach.

The esophagus doesn't simply act like an open pipe.

Muscular contractions called peristalsis help push food toward the stomach.

To demonstrate this idea, place the cracker-and-banana mixture inside a clean flexible bag or suitable tube model and gently squeeze it from one end toward the other.

Explain that the squeezing action is only a simplified demonstration.

The muscles in the digestive tract contract in coordinated waves to move food through the system.

Step 3: Create a Model Stomach

Now it's time for the most exciting part of the experiment—the stomach.

Place the crushed cracker and banana mixture inside a strong zip-top plastic bag.

Add a small amount of water and seal the bag securely.

Ask children to gently squeeze and knead the bag.

What happens?

The food mixture becomes softer and more blended.

This represents one aspect of what happens in the stomach.

The stomach muscles contract and mix food with gastric juices. These digestive fluids help break food down further.

Explain to children that the plastic bag isn't actually reproducing the chemistry of the stomach.

The real stomach contains acid and digestive enzymes, which are much more complex than this experiment.

The bag simply helps children visualize the mixing and mechanical breakdown of food.

Step 4: What Happens in the Small Intestine?

After food leaves the stomach, it enters the small intestine.

This is where much of the digestion and absorption of nutrients takes place.

The small intestine is remarkably long and contains specialized structures called villi and microvilli that increase the surface area available for absorbing nutrients.

For the experiment, spread the softened food mixture onto a clean tray or bowl.

Explain that the body is now separating useful nutrients from the remaining material.

The nutrients produced during digestion can be absorbed through the walls of the small intestine and enter the bloodstream.

This is one of the most important stages of digestion.

Step 5: The Role of the Large Intestine

After most nutrients have been absorbed in the small intestine, the remaining material moves into the large intestine.

The large intestine absorbs water and electrolytes from the remaining contents.

As water is removed, the material gradually becomes more solid.

For children, you can explain this using a simple analogy:

"Imagine squeezing water out of a wet sponge. The sponge becomes less watery. Something similar happens as the large intestine absorbs water from digestive contents."

The experiment doesn't need to physically reproduce this process. The goal is to help children understand the concept.

What Happens to the Food After Digestion?

By the end of digestion, useful nutrients from food have been absorbed and made available to the body.

These nutrients can provide:

  • Energy
  • Building materials for growth
  • Vitamins and minerals
  • Materials needed for repairing tissues

The material that the body cannot digest or absorb eventually leaves the body as waste.

This is why digestion isn't simply about "destroying" food. It is a carefully coordinated process that allows the body to obtain useful nutrients.

This simple digestive system activity introduces several important science concepts.

Children can learn that:

1. Digestion Begins in the Mouth

Chewing physically breaks food into smaller pieces, while saliva begins chemical digestion.

Water is removed from the remaining digestive material before waste is eliminated.

Questions to Ask Children

Turn the activity into an interactive science lesson by asking questions throughout the experiment.

Try asking:

  1. Where does digestion begin?
  2. Why do we need to chew food?
  3. What does the stomach do?
  4. Why is the stomach muscular?
  5. Where are most nutrients absorbed?
  6. What is the job of the large intestine?
  7. Why does the body need nutrients?
  8. What happens to material the body cannot use?

Encourage children to explain their answers rather than simply giving them the correct response.

Important Safety Tips

This is a simple activity, but adult supervision is recommended.

  • Use clean materials.
  • Wash hands before and after the experiment.
  • Don't allow children to taste experimental mixtures after handling them.
  • Use food that has not been contaminated by non-food materials.
  • Supervise young children when using scissors.
  • Clean the working surface after the activity.
  • Do not use actual stomach acid or household chemicals to imitate digestive juices.

The experiment should demonstrate digestion safely without trying to recreate the actual chemical environment inside the human body.

Why Hands-On Science Experiments Are Useful for Children

Children often understand complicated concepts more easily when they can see or physically demonstrate them.

A digestive system experiment transforms an abstract biology lesson into something tangible.

Instead of simply memorizing:

Mouth → Esophagus → Stomach → Small Intestine → Large Intestine

children can visualize what each stage is responsible for.

Hands-on activities can also encourage children to ask questions, make observations, and explain what they see. These are important skills in scientific thinking.

Conclusion

The digestive system simulation using crackers and bananas is an easy and engaging science experiment for children who want to understand how digestion works. Using familiar foods and simple household materials, the activity demonstrates important concepts such as chewing, food movement, stomach mixing, nutrient absorption, and water absorption.

However, children should understand that the model is only a simplified representation. Real digestion involves complex mechanical and chemical processes involving digestive enzymes, stomach acid, bile, the pancreas, and specialized structures throughout the digestive tract.

That's what makes this experiment a useful starting point rather than a complete model of human digestion. It gives children a simple visual framework that they can build upon as they learn more about human biology.

Most importantly, the activity turns a topic that can seem complicated in a textbook into an enjoyable hands-on children's science experiment. With a few crackers, a banana, and some curiosity, children can begin exploring one of the body's most fascinating systems—and discover what really happens to food after we eat it.
