Savitribai Phule: The Woman Who Opened the Door to Education for Girls in India

By Karthik Kumar D K | Published: Oct 4, 2026 7:58 AM IST
By Karthik Kumar D K , 4 October 2026
Savitribai Phule: The Woman Who Opened the Door to Education for Girls in India

There was a time in India when teaching a girl to read and write was considered an act of defiance.

For many women, education was not simply unavailable. It was actively denied. Social customs, caste discrimination and deeply rooted beliefs about the role of women kept large sections of society away from schools.

It was in this difficult social climate that Savitribai Phule picked up a book, became a teacher and walked towards a school every day despite knowing that people might abuse or humiliate her on the way.

Her story is not only about the first schools for girls. It is about what happens when education becomes a form of resistance.

Born on January 3, 1831, in Naigaon in present-day Maharashtra, Savitribai Phule went on to become an educator, poet and social reformer whose work challenged both caste and gender discrimination. NCERT recognises her as the first woman teacher of modern India.

Savitribai Phule's Early Life

Savitribai was born into a farming family at a time when opportunities for education were extremely limited, particularly for women and communities considered socially backward.

She was married to Jyotirao Phule while still very young. But her life after marriage took an unusual direction.

Jyotirao believed that education should not be restricted by gender or caste. He began teaching Savitribai at home. She continued her education and eventually received teacher training, preparing herself for a role that society had hardly imagined for an Indian woman of her time.

That education changed more than her own life.

It gave Savitribai the tools to become a teacher herself.

And soon, she and Jyotirao would take that education beyond their home.

The School That Changed Indian History

In 1848, Savitribai and Jyotirao Phule started a school for girls at Bhide Wada in Pune.

It was a radical decision.

At the time, educating girls was widely opposed, while children from oppressed communities faced additional barriers because of caste. The Phules believed that education was essential if society was ever going to change.

Savitribai became a teacher and headmistress.

The first group of students was small, but the significance of that classroom was enormous. The school provided girls with an opportunity to study at a time when formal education was largely controlled by social privilege.

The Phules did not stop with one school. Their educational work expanded, including schools for girls and children from communities that had historically been excluded from education. By the early 1850s, they were running multiple schools in Pune.

This is one reason Savitribai Phule's story remains important even today.

She did not simply argue that women deserved education.

She went out and taught them.

The Abuse She Faced on the Way to School

One of the most striking parts of Savitribai Phule's life is not what happened inside the classroom, but what happened on her way to it.

People opposed to her work reportedly mocked and harassed her. According to NCERT accounts, stones, mud, cow dung and other objects were thrown at her as she walked to school.

Imagine leaving home every morning knowing that the road to your classroom could become a place of humiliation.

Yet Savitribai continued.

There is a well-known account that she carried an extra sari because the one she wore could become dirty during these attacks. She would change before teaching and continue her work.

That detail captures something important about her character.

She did not need the opposition to disappear before she continued her work.

She simply continued despite it.

Education Was Only the Beginning

It would be easy to remember Savitribai Phule only as a pioneer of women's education.

That would make her story much smaller than it actually was.

Savitribai and Jyotirao worked on several social issues affecting women and marginalised communities. They challenged practices that left widows vulnerable and helped create support systems for women facing extreme social pressure.

They also established a home intended to prevent the killing of infants born to vulnerable women, particularly widows. The effort reflected their belief that social reform had to address real problems rather than merely talk about them.

Their work also challenged caste discrimination.

In 1868, the Phules opened their well to people who had been treated as "untouchable", directly challenging the social restrictions of the period.

For Savitribai, equality was not an abstract idea.

It had to exist in a classroom.

It had to exist at a water source.

It had to exist in the way society treated a widow.

It had to exist in the opportunities given to a child.

Savitribai Phule Was Also a Poet

Savitribai's contribution was not limited to teaching.

She was also a Marathi poet who used writing to encourage people to pursue education and challenge social inequality.

Her first poetry collection, Kavyaphule, was published in 1854. Her writing carried the same message that shaped her social work: education could help people question the conditions imposed upon them and seek a better life.

This makes her legacy particularly interesting.

She fought with a classroom, but she also fought with words.

Her poetry and speeches encouraged people who had been told that learning was not meant for them.

A Partnership That Became a Social Movement

Savitribai and Jyotirao Phule are often discussed together, and rightly so. Their partnership became one of the most important reform efforts in 19th-century Maharashtra.

But Savitribai should not be remembered merely as Jyotirao Phule's wife.

She developed her own identity as a teacher, organiser, writer and social reformer.

She continued the work even after Jyotirao died in 1890. Her actions during the final years of her life demonstrated the same courage that had defined her earlier work.

In fact, the final chapter of her life may be one of the strongest examples of her commitment to others.

Savitribai Phule and the Plague Epidemic

In 1897, Pune was facing a devastating plague epidemic.

While many people feared the disease, Savitribai worked to help those affected. She helped care for plague patients and continued serving people during the outbreak.

She eventually contracted the disease herself while caring for a patient.

Savitribai Phule died on March 10, 1897.

She died doing the kind of work that had defined her entire life — standing with people who needed help.

Why Savitribai Phule Still Matters

More than a century after her death, the questions Savitribai Phule confronted have not completely disappeared.

Girls' education has expanded enormously. Women today study in schools, colleges, universities and professional institutions across India. But access to education is still influenced by poverty, gender, geography and social inequality.

That makes Savitribai's story more than a chapter in history.

Her life reminds us that education is not simply about passing examinations or getting a job. For people who have historically been denied opportunities, education can mean independence, confidence and the ability to question the world around them.

Savitribai understood this at a time when the idea itself was considered threatening.

She walked to school when people wanted her to stop.

She taught girls when society questioned their right to learn.

She supported women when social customs left them vulnerable.

She challenged caste discrimination when doing so could bring serious social consequences.

And when an epidemic struck, she chose to help people even when it put her own life at risk.

That is why Savitribai Phule remains one of the most important figures in India's social reform history.

Her greatest achievement was not simply opening a school in 1848.

It was helping create the idea that a girl's education is not a favour given to her by society. It is her right.

And that idea, once placed inside a classroom, proved much harder to silence.
