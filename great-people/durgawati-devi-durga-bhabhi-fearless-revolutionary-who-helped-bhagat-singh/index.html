Durgawati Devi (Durga Bhabhi): The Fearless Revolutionary Who Helped Bhagat Singh

By Karthik Kumar D K | Published: Sep 27, 2026 10:48 AM IST
By Karthik Kumar D K , 27 September 2026
Durgawati Devi (Durga Bhabhi)

Durgawati Devi, popularly known as Durga Bhabhi, was one of the most remarkable women of India's revolutionary freedom struggle. She worked with the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association, helped Bhagat Singh escape from Lahore after the Saunders assassination, and later took part in revolutionary activities herself. Her story is not simply about courage; it is also about a young woman who stepped far beyond the traditional role society had assigned to her.

When the story of India's freedom struggle is told, names such as Bhagat Singh, Chandrashekhar Azad, Rajguru and Sukhdev naturally find their place at the centre. But behind the underground revolutionary network were several people whose contributions were less visible.

One of them was Durgawati Devi, better remembered as Durga Bhabhi.

She was not merely a revolutionary's wife or someone who happened to help Bhagat Singh. Historical accounts describe her as an active participant in the revolutionary movement, particularly in the network surrounding the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA). The Government of India's Azadi Ka Amrit Mahotsav records her as an active member of the Naujawan Bharat Sabha and notes her role in helping Bhagat Singh escape Lahore after the killing of British police officer John P. Saunders.

Who Was Durgawati Devi?

Durgawati Devi was born on 7 October 1907 in the Allahabad region of British India. Her early life was far removed from the image of the revolutionary woman she would eventually become.

She was married to Bhagwati Charan Vohra at the age of 11. Vohra later became an important member and ideologue of the revolutionary movement and was closely associated with Bhagat Singh and other members of the HSRA.

It was through this association that Durgawati Devi came into contact with the revolutionary world. The Vohra household in Lahore became a meeting place for young revolutionaries, and people such as Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Yashpal were part of that circle. Historian Kama Maclean's research describes how Durgawati gradually became involved in revolutionary activities rather than remaining simply a family member of someone in the movement.

Her revolutionary identity eventually became so strong that members of the organisation affectionately referred to her as Durga Bhabhi.

The Escape That Made Durga Bhabhi Famous

The most widely remembered episode in Durga Bhabhi's life came after the killing of John P. Saunders in Lahore in December 1928.

The revolutionary attack had been connected to the anger surrounding the death of Lala Lajpat Rai following a police lathi charge. After Saunders was killed, the British authorities launched an extensive search for the revolutionaries involved.

Bhagat Singh needed to leave Lahore quickly.

That was where Durga Bhabhi stepped into one of the most daring episodes of the revolutionary movement.

She agreed to travel with Bhagat Singh while presenting themselves as a married couple. Bhagat Singh changed his appearance and dressed in Western clothes. Durga Bhabhi travelled with her young son, Sachin, while Shivaram Rajguru accompanied them in the role of a servant. The disguise helped them pass through the railway system and escape Lahore.

The plan worked.

For Durga Bhabhi, however, this was not the end of her involvement. It was only one of several dangerous responsibilities she accepted during a period when the British police were aggressively pursuing members of the revolutionary network.

More Than Bhagat Singh's Companion

It is easy to remember Durga Bhabhi only because she helped Bhagat Singh escape. But doing so reduces her contribution considerably.

She was involved with the Naujawan Bharat Sabha, an organisation closely associated with the revolutionary youth movement. She also became connected with the HSRA's underground activities.

Her role included providing support to revolutionaries, helping maintain the underground network and taking risks that could have resulted in imprisonment or worse.

The historical record also shows that her involvement extended beyond providing shelter or assistance. Research into the revolutionary movement has highlighted how women such as Durga Devi participated in activities that challenged the assumption that the armed revolutionary movement was exclusively a man's world.

This is one of the most interesting aspects of her story. Durga Bhabhi was a young mother, yet motherhood did not prevent her from becoming involved in a movement that demanded secrecy, mobility and extraordinary personal courage.

The Loss of Bhagwati Charan Vohra

Durga Bhabhi's life became even more difficult in 1930.

Her husband, Bhagwati Charan Vohra, was working with revolutionaries who were attempting to develop explosives. On 28 May 1930, while testing a bomb on the banks of the Ravi near Lahore, he was fatally injured.

Vohra's death was a devastating personal loss. But Durga Bhabhi did not simply disappear from the revolutionary movement.

She continued to move through underground networks while British surveillance intensified. Historical accounts describe her hiding in different places and eventually leaving Lahore in disguise.

The Lamington Road Incident

One of the lesser-known episodes of Durga Bhabhi's life took place in Bombay.

On 8 October 1930, she was involved in an armed attack on Lamington Road. She fired at a European couple near a police station. The incident was later described in contemporary accounts as an important example of a woman directly participating in revolutionary violence.

Her involvement demonstrated that she was no longer simply assisting male revolutionaries from behind the scenes. She had become an active participant in the underground struggle herself.

After Bhagat Singh's Execution

The execution of Bhagat Singh, Rajguru and Sukhdev on 23 March 1931 marked another turning point.

Durga Bhabhi had already experienced the death of her husband. The execution of three revolutionaries with whom she had worked added another painful chapter to her life.

According to the Government of India's account, she later attempted to target Lord Hailey, the former Governor of Punjab, in an act of retaliation. Hailey escaped, although some of his aides were injured. She was subsequently arrested and sentenced to imprisonment.

These events illustrate how deeply the revolutionary struggle had shaped her life. For Durga Bhabhi, the freedom movement was not an abstract political idea. It had already cost her husband and several close associates.

The Quiet Life She Chose Later

Perhaps the most striking part of Durga Bhabhi's story is what happened after the revolutionary years.

Unlike some of the better-known freedom fighters, she did not spend the rest of her life constantly in the public spotlight.

After independence, she eventually settled into a quieter life. She worked as an educator and started a school for children in Lucknow. Later, she lived in Ghaziabad.

The woman who had once travelled secretly with Bhagat Singh and lived under the constant threat of arrest eventually became a teacher.

She died in Ghaziabad on 15 October 1999, at the age of 92. The Government of India records her life dates as 7 October 1907 to 15 October 1999.

Why Durga Bhabhi's Story Still Matters

Durgawati Devi's story adds an important dimension to the history of India's independence movement.

She reminds us that the revolutionary struggle was not built only by famous men whose photographs appear in history books. It also depended on women who carried messages, offered shelter, protected fugitives, travelled under assumed identities and, when required, directly entered the dangerous world of underground resistance.

Durga Bhabhi also challenges the tendency to describe women revolutionaries only in relation to the men around them.

Yes, she was Bhagwati Charan Vohra's wife.

Yes, she helped Bhagat Singh escape.

But she was also Durgawati Devi — a revolutionary in her own right.

Her journey from a young bride in Allahabad to an underground revolutionary, and finally to an educator living a relatively quiet life, is one of the lesser-known human stories of India's freedom struggle.

History often remembers the people who stood at the centre of the photograph.

Durga Bhabhi's story asks us to look at the people standing just outside the frame.

And sometimes, that is where some of the most extraordinary stories are found.
