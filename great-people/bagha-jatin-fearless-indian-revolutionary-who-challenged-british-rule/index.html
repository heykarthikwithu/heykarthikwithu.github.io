Bagha Jatin: The Fearless Indian Revolutionary Who Challenged British Rule

By Karthik Kumar D K | Published: Nov 1, 2026 9:24 AM IST
By Karthik Kumar D K , 1 November 2026
Bagha Jatin: The Fearless Indian Revolutionary Who Challenged British Rule

India's freedom struggle was shaped by countless individuals who were prepared to sacrifice their lives for a country that had yet to become independent. While some fought through peaceful movements and political campaigns, others believed that British colonial rule could only be challenged through organised resistance. Among these determined revolutionaries was Jatindranath Mukherjee, popularly remembered as Bagha Jatin.

Known for his extraordinary courage, physical strength and revolutionary leadership, Bagha Jatin became one of the most influential figures in Bengal's underground independence movement. His life was not simply about fighting the British. It was about building an organised movement, inspiring young Indians and creating a nationwide challenge to colonial rule.

His final battle near the Buribalam River in present-day Odisha in September 1915 remains one of the memorable episodes of India's revolutionary history.

Who Was Bagha Jatin?

Jatindranath Mukherjee was born on December 7, 1879, in Kayagram in the Kushtia region of undivided Bengal, now in Bangladesh. His father, Umeshchandra Mukherjee, died when he was young, and he was largely brought up by his mother, Sharatshashi Devi.

From an early age, Jatin displayed intelligence, determination and remarkable physical strength. He developed an interest in physical training, wrestling and other activities that helped him build the strength and discipline that would later become part of his public identity.

He was also influenced by the growing nationalist atmosphere in Bengal. British rule had created widespread resentment, particularly among educated Indians who increasingly questioned colonial exploitation and the absence of political freedom.

Jatin initially worked in government service as a stenographer. However, his growing involvement in nationalist activities gradually drew him towards the revolutionary movement.

How Did Jatindranath Mukherjee Become Bagha Jatin?

The name Bagha Jatin is associated with one of the most extraordinary incidents in his life.

In 1906, Jatin reportedly encountered a Royal Bengal tiger and fought the animal with a traditional dagger. Despite suffering serious injuries, he managed to kill the tiger.

The incident attracted considerable attention, and he became popularly known as Bagha Jatin. The Bengali word 'Bagha' means tiger.

The story became an enduring part of his identity. However, his importance in India's freedom struggle went far beyond his physical courage. What distinguished him was his ability to organise people, develop revolutionary networks and think strategically about challenging British authority.

Bagha Jatin and the Jugantar Revolutionary Movement

At the beginning of the twentieth century, Bengal had become an important centre of revolutionary nationalism. Groups of young Indians were organising themselves against British rule, believing that independence required more than petitions and political appeals.

Bagha Jatin became closely associated with the Jugantar revolutionary organisation, one of the most significant underground movements operating in Bengal.

Jugantar was not simply a group of individuals involved in isolated acts of resistance. It developed networks, trained young revolutionaries and worked towards a broader challenge to British authority.

Jatin emerged as one of its principal organisers and leaders. He believed that revolutionary activities needed discipline, coordination and a wider national vision.

He also developed connections with several important figures in the independence movement, including Sri Aurobindo and revolutionary leader Narendranath Bhattacharya, who later became known as M. N. Roy.

His leadership helped sustain revolutionary activities during a period when the British administration was actively trying to suppress nationalist organisations.

The British Government's Growing Concern

British authorities increasingly viewed revolutionary organisations in Bengal as a serious threat to colonial administration.

Bagha Jatin came under official scrutiny, and his involvement in revolutionary activities eventually brought him into the Howrah-Sibpur conspiracy proceedings.

He was arrested in connection with the case in 1910 and faced a prolonged legal battle. He was eventually acquitted in 1911.

However, the experience did not end his revolutionary activities. Instead, he continued strengthening the underground network and working towards a more ambitious plan against British rule.

His approach was different from simply organising small groups to carry out individual attacks. He wanted to create conditions for a much larger uprising.

Bagha Jatin and the Indo-German Conspiracy

The outbreak of the First World War in 1914 created an opportunity that Indian revolutionaries believed could weaken British colonial power.

Britain was deeply involved in the European war, and its military resources were stretched across different parts of the world.

Bagha Jatin and other revolutionaries saw an opportunity to secure international assistance, particularly from Germany, which was fighting against Britain during the war.

The plan involved obtaining weapons and ammunition from Germany and using them to support an armed uprising in India.

This effort became associated with the Indo-German conspiracy, also known as the Hindu-German conspiracy.

Revolutionary contacts worked across different countries to coordinate the proposed supply of arms. Narendranath Bhattacharya, later known as M. N. Roy, was among those involved in international efforts connected with the plan.

Jatin's role was particularly important in organising revolutionary networks within India and preparing for the expected arrival of weapons.

However, British intelligence became aware of the conspiracy. The planned uprising faced serious obstacles, and the expected arms supply did not materialise as intended.

The Battle of Buribalam: Bagha Jatin's Final Fight

By 1915, British authorities were actively pursuing Bagha Jatin and his associates.

Jatin and a small group of fellow revolutionaries had moved towards the Odisha region, hoping to coordinate activities connected with the planned arrival of German arms along the coast.

On September 9, 1915, British police forces confronted Jatin and his companions near Chashakhand, close to the Buribalam River in present-day Balasore district.

The revolutionaries were heavily outnumbered and faced a well-equipped police force. Nevertheless, they decided to fight rather than surrender.

The confrontation became known as the Battle of Buribalam.

Bagha Jatin was accompanied by four fellow revolutionaries: Chittapriya Ray Chaudhuri, Manoranjan Sengupta, Nirendranath Dasgupta and Jyotish Chandra Pal.

They fought against the British forces with limited ammunition. Chittapriya was killed during the encounter, while Jatin and other companions suffered serious injuries.

Eventually, the revolutionaries ran out of ammunition. The surviving members were captured.

Jatin was taken to the Balasore district hospital, where he succumbed to his injuries on September 10, 1915. He was just 35 years old.

His death marked the end of a remarkable revolutionary life, but his courage and determination continued to inspire generations of Indians.

Bagha Jatin's Legacy in India's Freedom Struggle

Bagha Jatin's contribution to India's independence movement deserves recognition beyond the famous story of his fight with a tiger.

He understood that challenging a powerful colonial administration required organisation, coordination and a clear objective. His efforts to connect revolutionary groups across geographical boundaries demonstrated an international outlook that was remarkable for his time.

His association with Jugantar and the Indo-German conspiracy also highlights how Indian revolutionaries explored opportunities beyond the country's borders to weaken British rule.

His final battle near the Buribalam River became a symbol of resistance and sacrifice.

Bagha Jatin is also remembered for his belief in collective action. His revolutionary journey involved numerous associates whose contributions were equally important to the movement. Remembering them helps us understand that India's freedom struggle was never the achievement of a single individual.

Today, Jatindranath Mukherjee remains an important figure in the history of Indian revolutionary nationalism. His life reminds us that the struggle for independence was fought by people with different ideologies, methods and visions, united by their opposition to colonial domination.

Bagha Jatin's story is ultimately the story of a man who refused to accept foreign rule as India's permanent destiny. He fought for a free country, knowing fully well that the struggle could cost him his life.

He did not live to see India become independent in 1947. But the courage, determination and revolutionary spirit he demonstrated remain an important part of the country's freedom movement.
